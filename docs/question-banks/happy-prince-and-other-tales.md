## 1. Book brief

- **Kind of book:** a collection of five short fairy tales (1888), written in
  a plain fairy-tale voice over a very adult set of ideas.
- **Readers in this app:** about 9–13. The sentences are easy; the endings are
  not.
- **Author's aim:** to write fairy tales in which the virtuous are not
  rewarded — the giving is real, and so is the failure of anyone to notice.
- **Big ideas:** **A** every tale has someone who gives everything and someone
  who does not see it · **B** beauty always costs somebody something · **C**
  self-regard (the Rocket, the Miller) is funnier and more damaging than
  outright wickedness · **D** the Giant is the only one who finds out in time.
- **What children love:** the swallow, the thorn, the wall coming down, and
  the Rocket going off with nobody there.

**Key scenes:** the Prince's ruby, sapphires and gold leaf · the nightingale
on the thorn · the rose in the gutter · the notice board and the endless
winter · the gap in the wall · the wheelbarrow that is never given · Hans on
the moor · the Miller at the funeral · the Rocket in the ditch and in the
daylight.

**Unsure / VERIFY:** none — all five plots were verified. The Giant's final
scene is referred to only obliquely in the summary, as the religious imagery
is not something a question should hinge on.

**Overlap with `the-happy-prince`:** the playbook flags that this collection
and the standalone title must not duplicate each other. This bank deliberately
spends **ten of its twelve questions on the other four tales**, and the two
that touch the title story ask about the sapphires and the two precious
things. When `the-happy-prince` is rewritten, it should avoid exactly those
two and take the ruby, the seamstress, the match-girl and the swallow's death.

## 3. Design for this book

- **Why this type mix:** a collection needs questions that prove the child
  read past the first tale, so matching and classification are both built to
  span the book: one matches an object to each of four tales, the other sorts
  six details between the two tales that are structurally twins (the Giant,
  who learns; the Miller, who does not). Ordering lives inside 'The Devoted
  Friend' because it has the clearest causal chain.
- **Coverage map:** the nightingale's method → q1 (idea B) · the rose thrown
  away → q2 (idea A) · the notice board → q3 · spring returning → q4 (idea D) ·
  the wheelbarrow chain → q5 (idea C) · four objects → q6 · Giant vs Miller →
  q7 (idea D) · the Rocket's ending → q8 (idea C) · was Hans a good friend →
  q9 (Evaluate, idea A) · invent a tale in the same shape → q10 (Create,
  idea A) · the sapphires → q11 · the two precious things → q12.

## 4. Proposed bank

**Three examples:**

1. *"Which tales are included in this collection?"* → **"How does the
   nightingale make a red rose grow?"** The old question is answerable from
   the contents page; the new one requires the second tale and its most
   shocking image.
2. *matching tales to their morals* → **matching four objects (a swallow, a
   thorn, a wheelbarrow, a damp firework) to their tales.** Morals are
   arguable and the old right-hand column read like a list of proverbs; the
   objects are unambiguous and each one is the pivot of its story.
3. *"Which tale did you like best and why?"* → **"Was Hans a good friend, or
   was he being used?"** A preference question replaced by the argument 'The
   Devoted Friend' is constructed to provoke — with a grading brief that scores
   both verdicts.
